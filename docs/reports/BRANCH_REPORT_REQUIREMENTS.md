# Lyne Branch Report Requirements

The owner's specification for the branch report (5 November 2026). Whatever writes
or generates a report, code or a model, works to this document.

## Purpose

Generate a management report that explains branch performance, supports operational decisions, and provides enough evidence for a branch manager to present the findings to executives.

The report must answer:

- What happened during the reporting period?
- How did performance compare with targets and the previous comparable period?
- Where and when did customers experience difficulty?
- How did services, staff, counters, sessions, and appointments perform?
- Which explanations are supported by recorded evidence?
- What should management change, and how should the results be measured?

Support three distinct editions: a 7-day operational review, a 30-day management review, and a 90-day performance review.

## Length and depth

Let the number of services, staff, operating days, material findings, and available records determine the final length.

Use these as initial design allowances, including appendices, rather than compulsory page counts:

| Edition | Suggested allowance | Main purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| 7 days | 15–25 pages | Explain recent operational problems and next week's actions |
| 30 days | 45–80 pages | Provide a comprehensive branch management review |
| 90 days | 65–110 pages | Evaluate sustained performance, recurring problems, and resource decisions |

A small branch or a period with limited data may produce a shorter report. A larger branch may require more pages.

Do not add repeated summaries, oversized charts, generic explanations, or empty pages to reach an allowance. Explain incomplete coverage prominently.

## 1. Cover and navigation

Give the cover its own page. Include the organisation, branch, report edition, exact reporting dates, prepared-for audience, requesting manager, generation date, and report identifier.

Start the table of contents on a new page. Include numbered chapters, meaningful subsections, and accurate page numbers. Make entries clickable in digital exports.

Include a list of figures and tables when the report contains enough of them to warrant one.

Use consistent chapter numbering, page numbers, running headers, figure captions, units, and table styles. Start major chapters on new pages, without giving every small subsection its own page.

## 2. Executive summary and decisions required

Prepare a self-contained summary that an executive can understand without reading the full report.

Include overall performance, the most material improvements and deteriorations, major customer impacts, important target failures, and the actions requiring management attention.

For each major finding, state the result, comparison, consequence, and relevant chapter reference.

Identify decisions explicitly. Examples include approving additional counter coverage, changing appointment capacity, investigating repeated incomplete visits, or correcting a data collection problem.

Scale the summary to approximately one page for the weekly edition and two to three pages for longer editions. Avoid compressing every chapter into a repetitive list.

## 3. Scope and reliability of the evidence

Explain the branch, services, channels, dates, timezone, operating hours, operating days, closures, and comparison period.

State whether the report includes the current partial day. Explain how visits that cross the period boundary are treated.

Distinguish between:

- Customer visits and individual service interactions.
- Unique identifiable customers and total visits.
- Completed service interactions and resolved customer needs.
- Queue no-shows and appointment no-shows.
- Zero activity and missing data.

Provide a compact coverage assessment showing which records are complete, partial, or unavailable.

All chapters must use consistent dates and filters. Current queue conditions may appear as a separately timestamped snapshot; they must not substitute for historical performance.

## 4. Branch performance overview

Present a reconciled view of arrivals, customers called, service starts, completed visits, abandonment, no-shows, cancellations, incomplete visits, and visits still open at the reporting cutoff.

Explain the relationship between these measures. A visit may have been called before being completed, so stages must not be added together as separate outcomes.

Include:

- Total visits and unique identifiable customers.
- Visits per operating day.
- Completion and abandonment rates.
- Average, median, and 90th-percentile wait.
- Average and median time at the counter.
- Total elapsed visit time where measurable.
- Percentage served within the applicable wait target.
- Comparison with the previous comparable period.

Explain the 90th percentile in plain language: 90% of eligible measured waits were at or below this value.

Show daily trends alongside period totals. Explain whether changes are widespread or concentrated in particular days, services, or hours.

## 5. Performance against targets

For each target, show its definition, applicable value, actual result, eligible record count, gap, previous-period result, and status.

Use percentage points for differences between rates. Identify whether higher or lower values represent better performance.

Show branch and company targets separately, with the rule determining which applies.

Include a target change history containing the previous value, replacement value, effective date, scope, and recorded reason or author where available.

Evaluate performance against the target effective at the time. If also comparing all results against the current target, label that as a separate comparison.

Explain whether apparent improvement came from operational results, a target change, a change in service mix, or incomplete evidence.

## 6. Individual service chapters

Give every active service a dedicated numbered subsection. Expand it into a longer chapter when its activity and findings justify the space.

Each service analysis should contain:

- Purpose and share of branch demand.
- Arrivals, completions, and recorded visit outcomes.
- Daily volume and previous-period change.
- Average, median, and 90th-percentile wait.
- Service duration and total elapsed visit time.
- Target attainment and breach counts.
- Abandonment and queue no-show rates.
- Incomplete visit reasons and unresolved work.
- Hourly and weekday demand patterns.
- Historical counter coverage and staffing.
- Material exceptions and recommended actions.

Compare services carefully. A lending consultation and a cash withdrawal have different workloads; shorter service time alone does not establish better performance.

The narrative must explain whether the principal issue concerns waiting, service duration, unfinished work, arrival concentration, or available capacity. State when the records cannot distinguish between possible explanations.

## 7. Staff profiles and counter performance

Provide a named profile for each staff member within the reader's authorised scope.

Include services handled, recorded counter assignments, active service days, completed interactions, service duration, service mix, and relevant incomplete or escalated work.

Where reliable records exist, include staffed hours, service hours, counter availability, and workload per staffed hour. Clearly define every denominator.

Compare staff within similar services and workloads. Discuss changes over time and potential support or training needs.

Do not rank staff solely by customer counts or speed. Do not attribute an entire queue's wait to the person who eventually served the customer.

Provide a separate counter analysis covering opening periods, service assignments, closures, and intervals when customers waited while suitable counters were unavailable.

Do not infer attendance, breaks, availability, or idleness from gaps between completed visits unless supporting records exist.

## 8. Demand and operating patterns

Analyse arrivals, waits, service duration, and counter coverage by hour and weekday.

Include an hourly demand chart, a weekday comparison, and a weekday-by-hour heatmap where sufficient observations exist.

Normalise weekday comparisons by the number of operating days observed. Account for partial hours, closures, and different opening schedules.

Identify:

- Opening queues and queues near closing.
- Repeated pressure periods.
- Differences between arrival peaks and waiting-time peaks.
- Service-specific bottlenecks.
- Intervals when demand exceeded observed service capacity.
- Underused periods that could support appointments or other work.

Investigate payday and month-end patterns only when dates can be identified reliably. State the number of observed cycles and distinguish a repeated pattern from a single busy day.

## 9. Customer journeys and incomplete visits

Explain what customers experienced from joining through to the recorded outcome.

Analyse entry channels, transfers, repeat visits, abandonment, queue no-shows, incomplete visits, and repeat visits following incomplete work.

Define the repeat-visit window. Separate repeat use of the branch from repeat attendance for the same unresolved matter where case links permit that distinction.

Show incomplete reasons by frequency, service, trend, and recorded responsibility where supported. Separate customer documentation issues, internal dependencies, external dependencies, referrals, and unknown reasons.

Do not assume that every repeat visit reflects failure. Do not treat a completed counter interaction as proof that the customer's underlying need was resolved.

Calculate unique-customer and repeat-visit measures only for records that can be linked reliably. Show identification coverage.

## 10. Client Cases

Report Client Cases as ongoing work with a lifecycle.

Include opening backlog, new cases, resolved cases, reopened cases, transfers, and closing backlog. Reconcile the movement between opening and closing balances.

Analyse case age, resolution time, overdue work, service categories, recorded reasons, ownership, and unresolved dependencies.

Separate outcomes for cases created during the period from resolutions of older cases.

Include selected anonymised examples only when they explain a material recurring issue. Describe the recorded timeline, outcome, dependency, and practical lesson.

Keep unnecessary personal details and unrestricted case notes out of management exports.

## 11. Sessions

Use Lyne's actual definition of a session and explain it clearly. Keep operational sessions distinct from customer visits and appointments.

Where recorded, compare planned and actual start and end times, service coverage, participating staff, available counters, visits handled, interruptions, and unresolved work at close.

Identify delayed starts, early closures, and recurring interruptions. Explain their relationship with queues only where the timelines support that interpretation.

If Lyne does not retain planned times or interruption reasons, state the limitation rather than estimating them.

## 12. Appointments

Analyse appointments scheduled to occur within the reporting period. Report booking creation trends separately if useful.

Include scheduled appointments, cancellations, reschedules, attendance, no-shows, late arrivals, punctual service starts, completion, and service duration.

Measure customer lateness separately from branch delays. Define cancellation and no-show rules, and account for rescheduled appointments without double counting them.

Compare appointment and walk-in outcomes within similar services and time periods.

Where capacity records exist, explain whether appointment slots were well used and whether appointment demand competed with walk-in demand during pressure periods.

## 13. Exceptions and recorded operating events

Provide a dated register of material events, such as outages, closures, unusually long waits, counter shortages, delayed sessions, or incomplete records.

For each event, show the affected services, duration, observed customer impact, recorded explanation, response, and follow-up.

Distinguish confirmed causes, plausible explanations, and unexplained associations. A long queue does not establish its own cause.

## 14. Forecast and staffing plan

Forecast only when sufficient historical coverage exists. With limited history, provide a clearly labelled planning scenario.

Show expected demand by relevant service, day, and time interval, together with an uncertainty range and important assumptions.

Translate demand into workload using service durations and existing backlog. Consider counter eligibility, staff skills, breaks, available hours, and appointment commitments.

Explain that a basic workload calculation estimates capacity; predicting a waiting-time target also requires accounting for uneven arrivals and service times.

Present feasible staffing options with proposed counter coverage, expected benefit, constraints, and review triggers. Label benefits as estimates unless they have been validated.

## 15. Recommendations and follow-through

Every recommendation must identify:

- The specific problem and supporting evidence.
- The proposed action.
- The responsible role.
- Timing and resource requirements.
- The expected result and its uncertainty.
- The measure and review date for assessing success.

"Reduce no-shows" is an objective. A recommendation must explain the intervention proposed to achieve it.

Prioritise actions by customer impact, urgency, feasibility, and strength of evidence.

Where previous action records exist, report progress and subsequent results. An action marked complete should not automatically be described as effective.

## 16. Daily detail and appendices

Include a dated record for every day within scope. Clearly distinguish operating days, closed days, zero-activity days, and days with missing records.

An expanded daily profile should contain opening hours, arrivals, outcomes, wait measures, target breaches, service mix, counter coverage, recorded events, and a short explanation of what made that day distinctive.

For a 30-day report, support a full daily appendix with one page per operating day when sufficient evidence exists. Use compact entries for closed days and days without enough activity to support a useful page.

For 90-day reports, default to weekly summaries and a complete daily table. Make an expanded daily appendix available separately.

Other appendices should include service and staff tables, target history, appointment and case summaries, metric definitions, calculation rules, exclusions, and data quality exceptions.

Provide large transaction-level exports separately when including them would overwhelm the readable report.

## How the editions should differ

| Area | 7-day review | 30-day review | 90-day review |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main question | What needs attention next week? | What explains this period's results? | What is changing persistently? |
| Time analysis | Daily results and recent exceptions | Daily results and weekly progression | Weekly trends and successive 30-day segments |
| Services | Concise review of every active service | Full service analysis | Sustained trends and recurring constraints |
| Staff | Workload and support needs | Individual profiles with context | Development and workload changes over time |
| Cases | Urgent and newly overdue work | Backlog movement and causes | Aging, recurrence, and resolution trends |
| Actions | Immediate operational adjustments | Prioritised improvement plan | Evaluation of interventions and resource needs |
| Forecast | Near-term planning | Next-period demand and coverage | Broader scenarios with uncertainty |

A rolling 90-day report must use its actual dates. Do not describe it as a calendar quarter unless those dates align.

Compare each edition with an explicitly stated previous comparable period. Use older history where available and relevant; do not imply that a 90-day window alone demonstrates annual seasonality.

## Writing the explanations

Open each chapter by explaining the question it addresses and why it matters.

After each substantive chart or table, develop the explanation through:

1. The most meaningful result, with its scale and eligible record count.
2. The comparison with a target, earlier period, or relevant baseline.
3. The location of the change within services, dates, hours, or customer groups.
4. The operational consequence.
5. The supported explanation, remaining uncertainty, and appropriate response.

Use connected paragraphs. Avoid repeating every table entry or mechanically adding five paragraphs when fewer would explain the finding.

A major chapter may need several paragraphs and supporting figures. A small daily exception may need only a short explanation.

For example:

"The average wait was 24 minutes, exceeding the branch target by four minutes. The period average alone does not show whether delays were widespread or concentrated in particular services. The service and hourly breakdowns should establish where breaches occurred before management changes counter coverage."

This explanation uses the available result while making the remaining analytical task clear.

## Requirements before release

Confirm that all sections use consistent scope and that totals reconcile wherever they describe the same population. Explain differences between visit counts, service interactions, unique customers, and staff credits.

Calculate period averages from the underlying eligible records or appropriate weights. Do not take an unweighted average of daily averages when daily volumes differ.

Show missing data as unavailable. Never convert it to zero or generate conclusions from empty charts.

Ensure every recommendation has supporting evidence, every forecast is labelled, and every table and chart has a clear period, unit, and definition.

Check the final exported pages for accurate contents references, readable charts, repeated table headers, sensible page breaks, and clipped or split content.

The finished report should allow a manager to trace an executive finding to the analysis and then to its supporting records.
